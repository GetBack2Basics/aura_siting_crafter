Precinct constraints
The Precinct presents a range of environmental, physical and infrastructure
constraints that are managed through the structure plan and addressed
progressively through staged development and site-specific assessment at the
development application stage.
Geotechnical stability and risk management – Development on the TSF and the
land located in the dam break inundation zone to the north is currently limited.
Soft alluvial soils located around the coastal wetlands in the north of the site
influence construction methods.
Environmental – Existing areas of high biodiversity values, high bush fire risk
and flood affected areas require further investigation to inform development
siting and design.
Sensitive uses – Interfaces between existing land uses, including residential
receivers and potential future industrial land uses will need to be managed to
mitigate negative noise, odour and visual impacts.
Infrastructure – Heavy vehicle access to parts of the site is currently limited
due to steep gradients, access through local residential areas and existing rail
crossings. The location of the existing oil pipeline, electrical transmission lines
and their associated easements, setbacks and design measures will need to be
considered.
Utility infrastructure – Viability and sequencing of the provision of utilities,
particularly to the West Lake sub-precinct, will need further investigation.

Structure plan
The structure plan (Figure 9) is the principal
spatial framework for the draft Master Plan. It
identifies the arrangement of developable areas,
environmentally constrained areas, recreational
areas, movement networks and key constraints
across the Precinct to guide development.
The structure plan outlines the spatial logic for
future development at a precinct scale, providing
a framework within which detailed design,
subdivision and development assessment will
occur. The structure plan is supported by technical
studies.
The following categories are used to describe land
within the structure plan:
•
Developable areas – Land that is potentially
suitable for development, subject to further
technical investigation and site-specific
assessment at the development application
stage.
•
Environmentally constrained areas – Land
identified as having biodiversity values or where
natural features (watercourses, wetlands, steep
slope, flooding) constrain development.
•
Recreation areas – Land potentially suitable for
active and passive recreation facilities,
balanced with protecting biodiversity and
cultural values.
•
Further investigation – Potentially developable
land that is constrained in the short to medium
term by geotechnical, dam safety, biodiversity
or flood risk factors, requiring resolution before
development can proceed. Development of this
land is contingent upon the completion
of relevant technical investigations and the
achievement of applicable staging triggers.

Strategic context
In 2024, the NSW Government launched an inquiry to investigate new and innovative approaches to
post mining land use to
make sure  mining sites continue to bring economic investment to regional
communities after mines close.
The findings of the
inquiry  ‘Report No. 53 Beneficial and productive
post mining land use’
were published including the NSW’s response to the inquiry. The NSW
Government  supported  all of  the recommendations in the inquiry.
Specifically, the following recommendations
are relevant to this project:
•
Recommendation 1
– Review of existing
legislation relating to post mining rehabilitation,
mining  closure  and future land use, to
facilitate  the transformation  of former mining sites.
•
Recommendation 6
– Focus  exploring immediate opportunities for two
pilot projects (Mt
Arthur  Mine and  Macquarie  Coal  Complex)  to identify  practical  impediments  and  necessary
legislative and regulatory changes
to enable land use change, and to
establish  a State -wide
model for post mining land use pathway.
The Rezoning Proposal was progressed by the Department in partnership with Australian
Government through NZEA, Council, other NSW Government agencies and Glencore.
Following the exhibition of the rezoning proposal, a recommendations
report will be prepared. The
report will make recommendations
on further amendments to the Environmental Planning and
Assessment Act 1979 (EP&A Act) and the Mining Act 1992. The recommendations
will be focussed on
enabling the post mining land use at the Macquarie Coal Complex, and more broadly across the
State as the energy transformation continues. The recommendations
will inform regulatory reform
to create a more streamlined planning pathway for redevelopment of post mining land. The refined
regulatory framework may still require additional technical studies to satisfy suitability of the land,
however, it is intended that there will be a clear pathway to undertake development consent on post
mining land.
The proposed amendments set out in this EIE can occur independently of the recommended
amendments to the EP&A Act and Mining Act.

Executive summary
This Utilities Engineering Report demonstrates that the Macquarie Coal Complex Precinct can be effectively
serviced to support its transition to a post-mining land use and provides a robust technical basis for rezoning.
The Precinct benefits from strong regional infrastructure connectivity, particularly in electricity, and access to
substantial on-site water resources, creating a high level of inherent servicing potential. While legacy mining
infrastructure and fragmented utility networks present constraints, these are considered manageable within a
staged delivery framework. Importantly, the site is uniquely positioned to adopt both traditional “business as
usual” servicing solutions and alternative, decentralised approaches that align with long-term sustainability
and industrial transition objectives.
Water Servicing
The Precinct is well positioned within Hunter Water’s regional supply system, with Grahamstown Water
Treatment Plant providing 246 ML/d of capacity and forecast spare capacity of ~17.5 ML/d in 2029,
exceeding the Precinct’s ultimate extreme day demand of ~16 ML/d. This demonstrates that, at a regional
scale, potable water supply is not a limiting factor to development.
However, constraints exist at a local level, with limited existing infrastructure across the site, fragmented
connections and the need to establish new trunk mains. These constraints are most pronounced in the
southern and western areas. The presence of substantial on-site stored water and proximity to recycled
water sources presents a key opportunity to supplement potable demand, enabling precinct-scale
alternative water systems (e.g. recycled water, raw water use). This creates a pathway to reduce
reliance on trunk augmentations and support a more resilient, lower-cost servicing strategy over time.
Wastewater Servicing
Wastewater represents one of the most significant constraints to development. The majority of the Precinct is
not connected to the Hunter Water wastewater network and historically relied on septic systems. This will
require the delivery of entirely new infrastructure including pump stations and rising mains to connect to
Edgeworth Wastewater Treatment Works.
Critically, Edgeworth WWTW is projected to reach capacity by 2029, with no confirmed timing or funding for
upgrades. This introduces a material risk to staging and delivery, as development is partially dependent on
external infrastructure upgrades.
At a precinct scale, this constraint strongly aligns with opportunities for decentralised servicing, including
on-site wastewater treatment, water recycling and reuse systems. These approaches could reduce reliance
on regional infrastructure in early stages and unlock development ahead of major augmentation works.
Electricity Servicing
Electricity is a major enabling service for the Precinct. The site has access to multiple voltage levels (11 kV
to 330 kV) and is located near key infrastructure including Newcastle BSP and Argenton Zone Substation.
Available capacity includes ~30 MVA at the 11 kV level and up to ~400 MW at the 132 kV network level,
providing significant long-term supply potential.
Total precinct demand is estimated at ~104 MVA (low case) to ~190 MVA (high case), which can be
staged progressively through feeder upgrades, substation augmentation, and potential new zone
substations.
Key constraints relate to the need for staged augmentation and potential long-term land take for substations
and transmission corridors. However, electricity also underpins a major opportunity to position the Precinct
as a low-carbon industrial hub, supporting electrification of industry, renewable energy integration,
microgrids and energy storage.

Gas and Energy Strategy
There is currently no gas infrastructure within the Precinct, with the nearest connection point located at
Racecourse Road and Blair Street. This limits feasibility for widespread gas reticulation, particularly in
western areas where lead-in distances are significant.
Rather than a constraint, this creates a strategic opportunity to minimise reliance on gas and prioritise
electrification and alternative energy systems, including renewable generation, battery storage and
emerging fuels such as hydrogen. Gas is therefore best treated as a targeted, case-by-case utility for
specific users rather than a core servicing backbone.
Telecommunications
Telecommunications is not a fundamental constraint to development but requires staged delivery. Baseline
mobile coverage is strong, and connections can be established from existing infrastructure at Powerhouse
Road and The Broadway.
Demand remains difficult to quantify at this stage, particularly given the potential for high-data users such as
data centres. The servicing approach relies on a developer-led NBN model, with pit and pipe infrastructure
delivered upfront and fibre rolled out incrementally.
Opportunities exist to develop a high-quality digital precinct, with scalable fibre networks, redundancy, and
integration of smart infrastructure. Additional mobile towers and bespoke connections for large data users
may be required as demand grows.
Key Cross-Cutting Constraint: Cockle Creek
Cockle Creek represents a major physical and servicing constraint across the Precinct. The creek creates a
barrier between the site and existing infrastructure networks, requiring new crossings for potable water,
wastewater and other utilities.
While feasible, these crossings introduce significant cost, delivery complexity and approval risk,
particularly where trenchless methods are required. This constraint reinforces the importance of staged
delivery and supports the case for decentralised servicing strategies, particularly in early development
phases.
Overall Conclusion
The assessment confirms that the Precinct can be serviced through a combination of traditional
infrastructure augmentation and staged delivery aligned with development uptake. While constraints such as
wastewater capacity, legacy infrastructure and Cockle Creek present challenges, they are not prohibitive to
development and instead provide a clear framework for infrastructure planning.
Importantly, many of these constraints align with and reinforce the opportunity to adopt alternative
servicing approaches, including decentralised water systems, electrification, renewable energy integration
and scalable telecommunications infrastructure.
On this basis, the report demonstrates that a viable servicing pathway exists to support the proposed land
uses and confirms that the Precinct is suitable for rezoning, with sufficient flexibility to accommodate both
conventional and innovative infrastructure solutions as development evolves.

Executive summary
This Geotechnical Engineering Report has been prepared to support the State-led rezoning of the Macquarie
Coal Complex (MCC) (the Precinct), and Teralba Southgate for post-mining land use. The report provides a
precinct-scale assessment of geotechnical conditions, constraints and risks, and establishes a risk-informed
framework to guide land use planning, infrastructure provision, and staging.
The Precinct and Teralba Southgate comprises a highly modified post-mining landscape characterised by
variable topography, anthropogenic ground conditions, and legacy mining impacts, including mine
subsidence risk, disturbed terrains, a Tailings Storage Facility (TSF) and areas of potentially deep alluvium.
These conditions result in spatially variable development constraints across the Precinct and Teralba
Southgate.
A multi-criteria geotechnical assessment has been undertaken, considering slope, geology, regolith depth,
soil landscapes, groundwater, salinity and acid sulfate soils. The assessment identifies a range of
constraints, from low to high, across the Precinct and Teralba Southgate and confirms that development
feasibility will depend on location, land use intensity and staging.
1.1
Key geotechnical constraints
The principal geotechnical constraints affecting the Precinct and Teralba Southgate include:
 Mine subsidence: all the sub-precincts are underlain by historic underground coal workings, resulting in
potential subsidence risk that must be considered in land use planning and foundation design. Potential
subsidence risks are not currently quantified as further studies are required. Development in these areas
must include geotechnical assessments to characterise the risk and potentially solutions such as grouting
of the former workings, if settlement has not already occurred.
 Tailings Storage Facility: the TSF comprises hydraulically placed tailings and fill materials with high
variability, uncertain composition, and long-term settlement potential. The area is currently undergoing
rehabilitation and remains a declared dam. It represents a high-risk area for development, with settlement
potentially occurring over decades.
 Alluvial and low-lying areas: land adjacent to Cockle Creek and its tributaries is characterised by deep,
compressible soils, shallow groundwater, flooding and acid sulfate soil risk, presenting significant
constraints to structural development.
 Disturbed and anthropogenic terrains: areas of fill and disturbed ground exhibit variable and uncertain
geotechnical properties, reducing bearing capacity and increasing settlement risk.
 Salinity and reactive soils: much of the Precinct and Taralba Southgate are affected by moderate to very
high salinity hazard and reactive clay soils, with implications for durability of infrastructure and long-term
maintenance.
1.2
Structure Plan sub-precincts and Teralba Southgate
 Lower constraint areas (e.g. Killingworth, West Lake, Teralba Southgate, Barnsley West and more
broadly the eastern portion of Cockle Creek) are suitable for early-stage development.
 Moderate constraint areas (e.g. environmentally constrained areas) require targeted investigation and
design responses prior to development, subject to subsidence risk investigation.
 Higher constrained areas, including the TSF within the Cockle Creek sub-precinct and floodplain
environments, are subject to long-term considerations for development potential.
Given that all the sub-precincts are likely underlain by historic underground mine workings and the entirety of
the Precinct and Terlaba Southgate is mapped as a declared Mine Subsidence District, all future
development will require approval from Subsidence Advisory NSW (SA) and may entail further site specific
mine subsidence investigations and assessments.

Executive summary
This Dam Engineering Report has been prepared by Aurecon on behalf of the Department of Planning,
Housing and Infrastructure (DPHI) to inform the State-led rezoning of the Macquarie Coal Complex (the
Precinct) for post-mining land use. The report provides a risk-informed planning framework to guide future

which remains regulated as declared dam.
The TSF represents a key constraint to short and medium development, due to the residual risk associated
with potential dam failure. Dambreak modelling has identified a downstream inundation zone, within which
development has the potential to increase risk to people (Population at Risk and Potential Loss of Life).
While current risk levels are well below regulatory thresholds, land use planning must not increase these
risks, in accordance with NSW dam safety requirements. A staged and adaptive planning approach is
therefore required.
Subsequently, post mining land use planning within and downstream of the TSF must adopt a risk-based
framework that:
 Avoids increasing population exposure within the dambreak inundation zone
 Requires periodic reassessment of dambreak risk as site conditions change
 Aligns developable land to demonstrated risk reduction
 Ensures compatibility between land use and the evolving residual risk profile of the TSF.
Key post mining land use implications
 Development constraints: Land within the dambreak inundation zone is constrained while the TSF
remains a declared dam. High-intensity or sensitive land uses which increase societal risk (e.g. residential
and industrial) are not appropriate in the short to medium term.
 Permissible interim uses: Low-intensity, transient or non-sensitive uses (such as open space,
conservation or passive recreation) may be considered, subject to maintaining flood conveyance and not
increasing risk.
 Infrastructure design: Infrastructure (including roads and utilities) within or crossing the inundation zone
must be designed above modelled inundation levels and resilient to flood impacts.
 TSF landform constraints: Development directly on the TSF is limited in the short and medium term due
to ongoing settlement and geotechnical constraints. Low impact uses that do not compromise the integrity
of the rehabilitated landform and capping are appropriate.
Staging and future development potential
The development potential of the Precinct will increase over time as rehabilitation progresses and dam-
related risks reduce. This is expected to occur through:
 Progressive lowering of water levels within the TSF (phreatic surface reduction)
 Completion of capping and landform stabilisation
 Updated dambreak and consequence assessments demonstrating reduced inundation extent and risk
As these milestones are achieved, land currently constrained may be progressively released for more
intensive development, subject to updated technical verification.

4.1
Investigation, design and construction of TSF
The surveillance report (ATCW, 2024) reports that geotechnical investigations at the TSF were conducted in
stages. The Phase One Study comprised of a review of existing conditions, embankments, in-situ tailings
characteristics, capping concept development, and an initial assessment of tailings consolidation to assist
with final landform design.
The Phase Two Study was completed in January 2018 and focussed on obtaining additional geotechnical
data. This included CPTu testing, drilling, sampling, and laboratory testing of the tailings and embankment
coarse reject materials. It is not clear if the investigations were also conducted at the NSD and eastern
embankment to establish the material parameters for these embankments and the underlying foundations.
The Phase Three Study addressed further works, specifically related to the Rejects Emplacement Area
(REA), that was recommended by ATCW resulting from the Phase Two Study, as well as recommendations
by a third-party reviewer.
Tailings
The fine-grained tailings exhibit compressibility and permeability values ranging from 0.06 to 0.4 m²/year,
indicating poor consolidation characteristics. Laboratory testing of the clay fraction confirms mineralogy
dominated by illite, swelling smectite, and kaolin. The in-situ strength profile of the underlying tailings
(including the capping layer) is not currently available and will require reassessment as part of final landform
design. For post-seismic assessment, a residual undrained shear strength ratio (Su,res/σ′vo) of 0.04 has
been adopted for the tailings and fly ash. This value corresponds to the 20th percentile of strengths derived
from CPT data within the weakest tailings zone adjacent to a hypothetical breach location.
Embankment Materials
The embankment materials comprise approximately 85% cobble- and gravel-sized particles, with the
remaining 15% consisting of sand and fines (silt and clay). For analysis, the embankment has been
subdivided into ‘new’ and ‘old’ rejects, with drained (effective) strength parameters applied to the new rejects
and undrained strength parameters applied to the old rejects. Although laboratory testing indicates broadly
similar particle size distribution and plasticity for both materials, undrained conditions are not considered
appropriate for the new rejects, as these have historically remained unsaturated.
The drained shear strength for the REA “new rejects” indicates an effective friction angle (ϕ′) of
approximately 38°, assuming zero cohesion. The undrained shear strength ratios range from 0.35 to 0.40 for
REA rejects and from 0.50 to 0.80 for old rejects. CPTu data indicate that the minimum undrained shear
strength of the coarse rejects consistently exceeds 20 kPa.

gradually decline. Seepage modelling undertaken by ATCW in 2020 indicates that the phreatic surface will
remain within the embankment for approximately 15 years following closure.

Executive summary
This Flooding and Water Cycle Management Report (This Report) presents a strategic flooding and water
cycle management (WCM) assessment for the Macquarie Coal Complex Precinct (the Precinct) and Teralba
Southgate to support a state-led rezoning and Master Plan. The assessment confirms that the Precinct can
be developed with a coordinated flooding and water cycle management strategy that supports future land
use while protecting people, property, infrastructure and the surrounding environment.
The assessment has been undertaken at a master planning scale using available flood studies, water cycle
data, site investigations and topographic analysis. It focuses on identifying key constraints, opportunities and
system behaviours rather than detailed design. On this basis, it demonstrates that flooding, stormwater,
water quality and emergency management outcomes are practically achievable across the Precinct through
an integrated, precinct-scale approach.
Flooding Strategy and Precinct Response
Flooding is a defining characteristic of the Precinct, driven predominantly by regional flooding from Cockle
Creek, supported by local overland flow processes and downstream tidal influences. These mechanisms
establish the spatial framework for development by identifying where land should be protected, where
development can occur, and where mitigation is required.
The Master Plan adopts a fundamental strategy of avoidance first, followed by selective, managed
encroachment in lower impact areas where development can be supported without increasing risk. This
approach preserves key floodplain functions while maximising viable development land.
The recommended flooding strategy is based on several core principles:
 Preservation of primary floodways and conveyance corridors
 Retention of flood storage areas and natural drainage pathways
 Direction of development toward areas of lower flood risk
 Integration of flood mitigation measures to support safe development
This approach ensures that development responds to the floodplain rather than working against it,
maintaining the hydraulic integrity of the system while enabling growth.
Protection of Surrounding Environment and Property
A key outcome of the assessment is that the proposed strategy protects both the environmental values of the
Precinct and the surrounding catchment, while avoiding adverse impacts on adjacent landholdings.
The Precinct drains to sensitive receiving environments including Cockle Creek, Burkes Creek, Diega Creek
and Lake Macquarie, which contain important ecological features such as riparian corridors, wetlands and
key fish habitat.
The master plan integrates these systems into the development framework by:
 Maintaining riparian corridors as functional flood conveyance and ecological buffers
 Protecting water quality through integrated treatment and management measures
 Avoiding increases in downstream flood risk or changes to flow behaviour
 Ensuring no material transfer of flood or drainage impacts to neighbouring land
This ensures that future development does not degrade environmental value or increase risk beyond the
site, a critical requirement for strategic planning and approval.

Introduction
SLR Consulting Pty Ltd (SLR) has been commissioned by the NSW Department of Planning,
Housing and Infrastructure (DPHI) to prepare a Biodiversity Assessment Report (BAR) to
support the proposed rezoning of the Macquarie Coal Complex and Teralba Southgate site
(hereafter referred to as the ‘subject land’) (Figure 1).
The Macquarie Coal Complex Precinct (the ‘Precinct’) is a former mining operation
comprising West Wallsend Colliery (underground mine), Westside Mine (open cut), the
Macquarie Coal Preparation Plant, Tailings Storage Facility and associated infrastructure.
The mining operation at the Precinct has ceased and the land is now subject to
rehabilitation works in accordance with existing requirements of the mining approvals. The
proposed rezoning of the subject land, which includes the Precinct, forms part of the NSW
Government’s investigation into future land use pathways for mining affected land.
This BAR has been prepared by SLR to accompany a State-led rezoning proposal (the
‘rezoning proposal’) in support of the proposed rezoning of the subject land and proposed
amendments to State Environmental Planning Policy (Precincts-Regional) 2021 (the
Regional Precincts SEPP) (the ‘project’). The proposed amendments to the Regional
Precincts SEPP are intended to give effect to the Macquarie Coal Complex Pilot Project
2026 Master Plan (the ‘Master Plan’) that will support future development of the Precinct.
Future development of the Precinct must consider the relevant aims and performance
criteria of the Master Plan. The Structure Plan for the rezoning proposal is provided in Figure
2.
Although the Master Plan under the Regional Precincts SEPP is limited to the Precinct, the
rezoning proposal applies to the entire subject land, which is comprised of the Precinct and
the Teralba Southgate site (Figure 1).
This BAR has generally been prepared in accordance with Stage 1 of the Biodiversity
Assessment Method (BAM) and provides an assessment of the existing biodiversity values
in the subject land and the potential future impacts associated with the rezoning proposal.
1.1
Purpose
The purpose of this BAR is to describe the current biodiversity values of the subject land and
provide an indicative assessment of the potential impacts of the project on flora and fauna to
support the rezoning proposal. In particular, this includes threatened species, populations
and ecological communities that are listed under the NSW Biodiversity Conservation Act
2016 (BC Act) and/or the Commonwealth Environment Protection and Biodiversity
Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC Act). In addition, this BAR identifies future ecological
assessments and associated avoidance and mitigation measures that may be required for
future development within the subject land that would be facilitated by the future rezoning
and associated Master Plan.
The findings of this BAR have informed and shaped the Master Plan for the Precinct.
Nonetheless, it is recognised that detailed technical assessments under the BC Act and
EPBC Act are required for future development applications in the subject land, including
assessments in accordance with Stage 1 and Stage 2 of the BAM.

1.1
Purpose
This bushfire technical assessment has been prepared to support the post-mining land use planning and
master planning process for the Macquarie Mine precinct. The assessment forms part of the broader technical
workstream being undertaken to inform the development of the Draft Structure Plan and establish a strategic
planning framework for future land use opportunities across the site.
Consistent with the Post Mining Land Use (PMLU) program, the assessment is deliberately scoped at a
rezoning and master planning level rather than a development application level. The purpose of the
assessment is not to undertake detailed bushfire design, Bushfire Attack Level (BAL) assessments,
emergency management planning or development-specific compliance analysis. Rather, the assessment
seeks to identify the key bushfire opportunities, constraints and resilience considerations that may influence
future land use allocation, development capacity, staging and infrastructure planning.
The assessment adopts a constraints-led and risk-based planning approach, recognising that bushfire is one
of several factors that will influence the long-term suitability of land for future development. Consistent with
the Enquiry by Design (EbD) process, bushfire considerations are assessed alongside environmental
constraints, rehabilitation objectives, biodiversity outcomes, infrastructure requirements, transport networks,
servicing opportunities, cultural heritage considerations and broader strategic planning objectives.
1.2
Assessment Principles
The assessment has been guided by the core principles established for the PMLU program, with a focus on
providing sufficient information to support strategic planning and master plan decision-making while avoiding
unnecessary duplication of future development assessment processes.
The assessment therefore seeks to:
•
identify bushfire constraints that may influence the sequencing, configuration and intensity of future
development;
•
leverage existing information including mine closure planning, rehabilitation strategies, previous technical
investigations, bushfire mapping, fire history and outcomes from the EbD process;
•
undertake targeted verification of key bushfire considerations where necessary to inform strategic planning
decisions;
•
assess opportunities and constraints in a practical manner, recognising that future development may be
appropriate where bushfire risk can be effectively managed through land use planning and infrastructure
provision;
•
integrate bushfire considerations with other technical disciplines through precinct-scale analysis and
spatial planning; and
•
establish clear separation between strategic planning requirements and matters that should be deferred to
future development application, subdivision and detailed design stages.
1.3
Role of the Bushfire Assessment
The assessment is intended to provide the bushfire evidence base necessary to support the Draft Structure
Plan and future rezoning considerations. Specifically, the assessment seeks to:
•
understand the broader bushfire landscape affecting the Macquarie precinct;

assess the strategic feasibility of accommodating Asset Protection Zones (APZs), access arrangements
and firefighting infrastructure;
•
identify bushfire resilience measures that should be incorporated into the Structure Plan;
•
inform development staging and infrastructure sequencing;
•
establish bushfire-related objectives and performance criteria suitable for inclusion within the Master Plan;
and
•
provide a framework for future engagement with the NSW Rural Fire Service and other relevant agencies.

1.4
Integrated Planning Approach
The bushfire assessment forms one component of a multidisciplinary planning process involving urban
design, engineering, environmental, heritage, economic and statutory planning specialists. Through the EbD
process, bushfire considerations are integrated with flooding, biodiversity, rehabilitation, transport,
infrastructure, contamination, servicing and land use planning considerations to ensure that future
development opportunities are assessed holistically rather than in isolation.
This integrated approach recognises that the future success of the Macquarie precinct will depend on
balancing environmental, social, economic and resilience outcomes. Bushfire therefore represents both a
constraint and a design consideration that must be addressed through the structure and layout of future

Executive summary
This Traffic and Transport Report has been prepared to support the State led rezoning and associated
master planning of the Macquarie Coal Complex (the Precinct) and Teralba South Gate sub-precinct
(collectively referred to as the Site), a large post mining landholding west of Cockle Creek in the Lake
Macquarie Local Government Area. The Site would be located between Teralba and Barnsley, framed by the
M1 Pacific Motorway to the west, the B89 George Booth Drive and Main Road corridor to the north and the
B53 arterial to the east. Wakefield Road is the key north-south spine through the area.
The Site would comprise approximately 211,100 m2 of large format warehousing across the Cockle Creek
and Killingworth employment cores, and a mixed-use core of around 18,100 m2 at West Lake. At full
development, the Site is anticipated to generate in the order of 12,470 daily trips of which 1,030 trips are
expected to be heavy vehicles and 300 RAV 19 metre B-doubles. Peak hour volumes are expected to be in
the order of 1,400 vehicles in the AM peak and 1,300 in the PM peak.
Midblock and intersection performance was reviewed across the base year and the short, medium and long
term, with and without the Precinct and a new Cockle Creek crossing. In the base year the network is
expected to operate at Level of Service (LoS) A to B, with only the Northville Drive corridor toward the B89
approaching LoS C to D.
Background growth is expected to drive the Northville Drive corridor to LoS E by the long term. The southern
and western network would remain at LoS A to C with the Precinct throughout. The Northville Drive and The
Weir Road intersections on the northern approach to the B89, would have adjoining links reaching LoS D in
the short term and level of service F by the long term and consequently have been identified for potential
upgrade, with proportionate contributions where they reach capacity under background growth.
Cockle Creek is a major line of severance for access in the wider area resulting in an already congested
network to the southeast of the precinct. Reducing future vehicle demands on existing creek crossings
through traffic management, mode shift, infrastructure upgrades and potential new infrastructure will be
appropriately incorporated within the onward planning for the precinct.
Three structural findings drive the network strategy:

◼ The Five Islands Road crossing of Cockle Creek is the only principal crossing and already operates at or
near capacity. Therefore, the potential for a second crossing is a consideration in managing Precinct

Introduction
SLR Consulting Australia Pty Ltd (SLR) has been engaged by Aurecon Pty Ltd (Aurecon) on
behalf of the Department of Planning, Housing and Infrastructure (DPHI) to undertake a
Noise and Vibration Technical Assessment (NVTA) to inform planning considerations
associated with the proposed state-led rezoning of the Macquarie Coal Complex.
The Macquarie Coal Complex Pilot Project 2026 (the Master Plan) details the overall
planning direction for the proposed rezoning to support coordinated transition from post
mining land use to alternative employment generating land uses.  This report outlines noise
constraints and establishes the method for determining specific noise performance criteria
for the structure plan by considering the proposed land uses and their impact on existing and
future sensitive land uses and presents in-principal noise mitigation and management
strategies to ensure the acoustic amenity of the local community and individual sensitive
land uses surrounding the proposed rezoning are not adversely impacted.
SLR is a member of the Australian Acoustical Society (AAS) and a member firm of the
Association of Australasian Acoustical Consultants (AAAC).  The following report uses
specialist acoustic terminology. An explanation of common terms is provided in Appendix A.
1.1
Precinct Description
The Macquarie Coal Complex Land Transformation Precinct forms part of the NSW
Government’s investigation into future land use pathways for mining affected land. The
Master Plan represents a future-focused commitment to a transition for mining-dependent
communities. It is complementary to the NSW Government’s broader regional priorities and
investment pipeline, as well as supporting Lake Macquarie City Council’s emphasis on
investment in long term public value for the community.
The Rezoning Proposal applies to 1,160 hectares of land and includes land at Macquarie
Coal Complex (the Precinct) and Teralba Southgate sub-precinct, collectively referred to as
the Site.
Macquarie Coal Complex
The Precinct sits adjacent to the Lake Macquarie Northwest Catalyst Area and is bounded
by the suburb of Barnsley to the north, Cockle Creek and the Central-Coast Newcastle main
passenger and freight train line to the east, Teralba and Wakefield to the south and
Killingworth to the west. The M1 Pacific Motorway and Sugarloaf State Conservation Area
are also located within in close proximity.
Teralba Southgate
Teralba Southgate sub-precinct site is located approximately 1.5km southeast of the main
Macquarie Coal Complex site, 400m from Teralba Train Station and 250m from Booragul
Train Station. Teralba Transport Oriented Development site (TOD) is adjacent to the north,
and Booragul TOD adjoins the south of Teralba Southgate. Teralba Southgate is
approximately 7 hectares and is comprised of existing warehouses, sheds and hardstand
areas associated with historical mining uses of the site.

Amenity, caused by:
o odour emissions.
o dust deposition on surfaces such as clothes, dwellings, and vehicles.
o visible plumes, and more widely, visibility.

This document answers frequently asked questions about the Macquarie Coal

What is Post Mining Land Use?
The Macquarie Coal Complex Transformation Precinct (the Precinct) is part of a broader Mine Land
Reuse initiative supported by Federal, State and Local Governments, aiming to deliver improved
economic and employment outcomes on former mine sites. It responds to the Parliamentary Inquiry
into Beneficial and Productive Post-Mining Land Use, particularly Recommendations 1 and 6, which
call for legislative reform that will enable the successful transformation of former mining land to
new employment uses.
In July 2025 the NSW Government committed to undertake a pilot project at the
Macquarie Coal Complex in Lake Macquarie Local Government Area to demonstrate how alternative
post-mining land uses can deliver opportunities for the region through coordinated relinquishment
and redevelopment of former mining land.
Lake Macquarie City Council (Council) were allocated $2.5M in funding from the Federal
Government’s Net Zero Economy Authority (NZEA) with in-kind support from the State Government
to undertake a master planning process for the Precinct.
The Precinct is led through a collaborative partnership between Department of Planning, Housing
and Infrastructure (the Department), NZEA, local government and industry. This collaboration
between all three levels of government and the private sector represents one of the most significant
examples of early, coordinated post mining land use planning in NSW.
Where is the site located?
Located on Awabakal Country within the Lake Macquarie Local Government Area, the Precinct
comprises approximately 1160 hectares of land across the suburbs of Teralba, Killingworth,
Wakefield, and Barnsley. The Precinct forms part of the Lower Hunter region and is strategically

Future investigations will be required to consider the environmental and cultural outcomes

How does the rezoning impact Glencore’s rehabilitation obligations?
Glencore has ceased mining operations, with the site advancing through rehabilitation works in
accordance with the approved Macquarie Coal Rehabilitation Management Plan (2022). Glencore
will continue to meet their rehabilitation obligations – this process does not bypass their legislative
requirements.
What type of development does the Master Plan envision?
The Master Plan will deliver a diverse range of uses across the Precincts, complemented by
recreation, tourism, community and other development uses that deliver broader public value and
economic growth.
The following employment is anticipated across the Precinct:
•
General industrial
•
Logistics and warehousing
•
Light industrial
•
Circular economy industries
•
Renewable energy generation and storage
•
Advanced industry and technology
Other development opportunities may be identified during exhibition.
